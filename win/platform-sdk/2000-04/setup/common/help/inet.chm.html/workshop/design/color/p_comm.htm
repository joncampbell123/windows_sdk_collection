Successful Color Communication

May 5, 1998

from Pantone, Inc.
Raymond Pirouz

Simply using color is not enough. To achieve successful color communication on your Web site or Web advertisement, you need to do two things:

  • Produce and deliver consistent color across platforms and browsers
  • Use color strategically

Why Strive for Consistency?

When you create artwork for distribution on the Web, know that your audience consists of a mixed bag of PC, Macintosh, UNIX, and WebTV users whose means to the Internet vary.

Not all of the above mentioned platforms share the same color look-up tables (CLUTs), which define colors within their respective system palettes. This can mean big trouble for your Web colors if there are no standards.

Fortunately, the founders of the original Mosaic browser discovered a "platform-safe" set of colors (216 to be exact) that communicates consistently across all platforms and browsers.

To deliver consistent color to your audience, you must first be able to select from a consistent palette of colors within your own system. If your computer system is not calibrated to display colors properly, your ability to select from a solid base of colors may be compromised.

One available tool for calibrating the colors of on-screen images is Pantone, Inc.'s PANTONE Personal Color Calibrator™ .

Once your system is calibrated, you are one step away from creating consistent color on the Internet… Then, to select your colors, you can use the PANTONE ColorWeb , which can help you make on-screen selections from the 216 Internet-safe colors.

Strategic Color Communication

Use color to establish a mood

Black can communicate raw, somber or slick. White backgrounds tend to communicate simplicity -- clean, organized, corporate, or sterile. Be careful when using very bright colors, such as orange or yellow, for your background color. These colors can make it difficult to read. If the purpose of your site is to create an energetic mood -- and you do not want people reading much of anything -- then by all means, use bright colors.

If you are communicating up-to-the-minute medical information to an older crowd, you may want to use a white (or gray) background with large (HTML size 4 or larger) black type that will be easy for the elderly to read.

If your goal is to deliver concert information to a teen audience, you may want to use bright colored backgrounds with contrasting type (orange background with white type, for example). If your goal is to sell products and services to a specific audience, you may want to use a background color appropriate to your industry (green for agriculture, blue for marine, white for medical, pastels for cosmetics, and so forth).

Carefully select a background color scheme that will establish the type of mood specific to the kind of information you'd like to communicate to your audience. There really are no hard rules to follow here. The color you ultimately choose will be based on:

  • The kind of mood you'd like to create for your Web site
  • The kind of information you are communicating
  • Your audience

Avoid "default" colors unless they apply. On the Web, gray is considered an almost "default" color. The use of gray is highly appropriate if you are trying to establish a serious mood. For example, if the goal of your Web site is to communicate the newest HTML spec or mathematical formulas, you can get away with using a gray background. Gray backgrounds can be considered boring if you do not balance them out with white (as well as black or orange type, for example). Blue for text links and boxes should be avoided as they are "default" color settings on many "non-designed" Web pages. Unless you intend to use a gray background, or the link-color blue matches your site's color-scheme, you should take care to select colors that apply to the subject-matter of your Web site.

Use color to attract

A red box on a solid white background will immediately attract the eye. The strategic use of color can help you lead your viewers' eyes throughout your Web pages and advertisements.

Use color to categorize

Color can help communicate a complex navigational system or assist your users in determining where they are within your site structure. Color is an awesome ally in categorizing and defining unique sets of information for your audience.

Use color with strategic intent

Always use color for the sake of achieving your overall communication goal. Color for the sake of color can be nice, but the strategic use of color will help you deliver consistent communication to your audience.

For example, if your goal is to sell products or services, take care to select colors that create a pleasant environment for the viewer. Remember that you are trying to sell something. The last thing you want is for your viewer to feel uncomfortable or turned off by your colors, and to leave. If you look around at the commerce sites littering the net, 99.9% have a white background. Why? White is safe. White communicates clean, organized, corporate; white creates a pleasant environment. Does this mean you should limit yourself to white when trying to sell products on the Internet? I think not. White is a safe starting place, but if you're a bit more adventurous, you can elect to experiment with your audience. Try a black background (to communicate a slick store) with products displayed on white circles, for example. This technique uses white in an unexpected way. The beauty of the Web is that you can always use surveys to ask your viewers what they like (or dislike) about your site. Believe me, they'll tell you if your color choices are way off.
