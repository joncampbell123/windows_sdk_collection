Color Management FAQ

October 30, 1997

from Pantone, Inc.
Richard Herbert

Editor's note: The following FAQ previously appeared in the June 1997 issue of Graphic Design: USA, and is republished with permission.

How important is color management on the World Wide Web?

Corporations invest millions of dollars to ensure that their corporate logos and and product-identity colors are accurate in whatever medium they are being used -- whether traditional or electronic. The Internet is simply another medium for publishing information. Consider the Coca-Cola Corporation and the investment it has made making sure that Coca-Cola red is exactly the same color wherever it is used throughout the world. Coca-Cola red is a copyright-protected company asset that must be reproduced accurately wherever it is seen.

In addition to brand equity and corporate identity, color is an integral component of electronic commerce. Clothing and cosmetic companies are making products available through online catalogs. For these companies, color is an essential factor in the sale of a product. "That's not the color I wanted," is the standard complaint heard by companies such as J. Crew and Land's End. In fact, "wrong color" is the number one reason for the return of clothing and products purchased through catalogs. Without color management -- the accurate rendering of color -- vendors of color-critical products will have a difficult time developing a profitable online business.

What is the current state of color management on the Web?

Color management is not currently a gating issue for content providers or Web surfers, but its importance is growing steadily. As with any new technology, issues crucial to long-term success must be addressed as a priority. In the case of the Internet, the issues were transmission bandwidth, the development of techniques to display graphics quickly, and transactional security. Now that these are being resolved, we can logically move to the second wave of advancement for the Web: technology devoted to accurately rendering the color of Web page content.

Similar to the inception of the desktop color revolution, people were initially delighted to be able to print in color. Today we have higher expectations and now expect more than just being able to print in color. We expect "good" color. The same holds true for the Web. The expectations of users, content providers and browsers have risen so that simply displaying a logo or a product is not good enough -- now, the color must be accurate.

Are there any industry forums exploring Internet color standards?

Two associations that have been very active in this arena: the International Color Consortium (ICC), and Hewlett-Packard and Microsoft, who have proposed the adoption of support for the standard RGB color space based on a modified HDTV specification, called sRGB.

The ICC was established to create, promote, and encourage the standardization and evolution of a vendor-neutral, cross-platform color-management system. The ICC defined a standard that accurately describes the color characteristics of a device and how that "profile" can be translated to any other ICC-described device.

sRGB complements current color management strategies by establishing an agreed upon default method of color specification. sRGB uses a device-independent color definition that can be assumed by the operating system or peripheral devices such as color printers in the absence of another device profile.

Are there any vendors providing Internet color-management solutions?

Not many vendors are lending their expertise to Internet color management solutions. Silicon Graphics, Inc. (SGI) and Pantone are two companies that have been at the forefront of utilizing color-management technology to obtain the best possible results when working with color content on the Internet.

SGI's Cosmo Color technology enables a Web publisher to ensure that color is being accurately reproduced at the client end. Designed to work with Silicon Graphics' WebFORCE servers and Java-aware browsers, Cosmo Color consists of two components: a monitor characterization component and a server-based program.

Pantone offers color-management solutions for both Web designers and Web clients with its array of tools -- including Pantone ColorDrive with Web Tools and Pantone ColorWeb, which is based on the 216 "Internet-safe" colors for designing Web pages, and can be viewed cross-platform.

How does the move to Internet publishing impact traditional publishing? Are they complementary?

Internet publishing is simply another vehicle for providing content. It both complements and competes with traditional publishing. It's complementary in that it provides an immediate way of presenting information, updating content and reaching a broader audience with a company's messages. The Internet competes with traditional publishing by reducing the need for printed materials that can be more conveniently viewed online, such as brochures. For the publishing market, it can also serve as a useful addition to the day-to-day design process, speeding the client-approval cycle by providing a way to soft proof images.

Do you believe that for each Internet designer there is one less print designer?

I think that print designers are re-inventing themselves to become Web designers in an effort to better serve their customers. The death of the printed document has been overstated by Internet proponents, but designers need to be able to service both digital and hard copy worlds to be competitive. Keep in mind that a minority of the population is connected to the Internet. Therefore, documents still need to be portable, and not confined to a computer screen. Besides, with the vast amount of content available on the Internet, a lot of it is destined to be printed, simply because it is easier to distribute in many cases.

Are designers re-purposing Web designs for print?

I think that Web designs are being designed for Web viewing. Elements such as frames and graphic-intensive backgrounds often make Web content unsuitable for print. One way to repurpose Web designs for print with a minimum amount of overhead and effort is to develop standard templates that take into consideration the dual nature of the content being created.

Richard Herbert, senior vice president of Pantone, Inc., is the founder of Pantone's Electronic Color Systems division. A 14-year veteran of computer color matching, color science, and computer graphics, he is the pioneer behind Pantone's Internet color-management solutions.
